Global · Currency
China's New Funding Power
Beijing Is Building the RMB Into a Global Financing Currency
China has always preferred to operate from a position of strength, and the RMB is becoming another tool to achieve that. With domestic interest rates well below dollar borrowing costs, Beijing is using its deep financial markets, large savings pool and expanding payment infrastructure to make the RMB more attractive to foreign borrowers. At the centre of this push are Panda bonds, RMB-denominated bonds issued by foreign entities in mainland China, and Dim Sum bonds, RMB-denominated bonds issued offshore, mainly in Hong Kong. Beijing is also expanding CIPS, its RMB-focused cross-border payment and settlement network, along with Bond Connect, which links overseas investors with China's domestic bond market, and Swap Connect, which supports cross-border interest-rate risk management.
Cheap Money Is Pulling Borrowers In
The biggest attraction is cost. China's 10-year government bond yield is around 1.68%, versus roughly 4.5%-5.5% in dollar markets. The China-US 10-year yield spread has exceeded 100 bps since June 2023 and reached around 300 bps in January 2025. That gap is showing up in issuance. Panda bond issuance reached RMB160 billion in H1 2026, up 68% YoY. By July 31, issuance had reached RMB195.7 billion, already above the full-year 2025 total. Outstanding Panda bonds crossed RMB500 billion, reaching RMB506.4 billion, up about 36% YoY. A five-year BNP Paribas Panda bond issued in March 2026 carried a 2.05% coupon, compared with roughly 5% for comparable offshore dollar funding.
Foreign Borrowers Are Joining
Foreign participation is rising fast. Foreign issuers made up 50% of Panda bond issuance by end-July 2026, up from 39% in 2025 and 27% in 2023. Foreign governments alone issued nearly RMB20 billion in H1 2026, compared with RMB7 billion in all of 2025, showing how quickly the market is gaining traction. The cost advantage is a key driver. Slovenia, Pakistan and Kazakhstan completed their first sovereign Panda deals, while Pakistan raised RMB1.75 billion at 2.5%, versus 6.975% for a comparable three-year dollar borrowing earlier in 2026.
Beijing Is Building More Than a Bond Market
Panda bonds provide an onshore RMB funding channel, while Dim Sum bonds provide an offshore channel. Together, they are helping China move the RMB from a trade currency towards a broader funding and investment currency. As more foreign borrowers use RMB funding, they also become more connected to RMB liquidity, hedging and settlement systems. A larger borrower base can deepen these markets further and make RMB funding more familiar and accessible internationally. The aim is increasingly clear: make the RMB easier to borrow, invest, hedge, settle and hold across borders.
The Timing Is Becoming More Interesting
China's RMB internationalisation push has been underway for years, but the funding landscape is changing. Japan is no longer the ultra-cheap funding base it once was. Its 10-year government bond yield has crossed 3% for the first time since 1996, making the traditional Japanese funding advantage less compelling. This could push borrowers and investors to explore alternatives. China stands out with a 10-year yield of around 1.68%, but replacing Japan is not just about cost. Liquidity, hedging markets, capital controls and investor confidence still make the RMB a less mature alternative.
The RMB Is Growing, But the Dollar Still Dominates
The RMB is gaining ground, but the dollar remains firmly dominant. The RMB accounted for 3.8% of international payments at end-2024 and around 2% of global official FX reserves as of July 2026. Its SWIFT share peaked at 4.7% in July 2024 before falling to 2.75%, suggesting that international adoption has been uneven. At the same time, CIPS is expanding rapidly, with direct participants rising from 79 banks in January 2023 to 210, while transaction volumes reached record highs. The takeaway is not RMB replacing the dollar, but China steadily building a parallel funding and payments ecosystem alongside it.
Opening the Door, Keeping the Keys
China has many of the ingredients needed for a global currency: deeper bond markets, cross-border payment infrastructure, broader foreign access and RMB liquidity facilities. But capital controls remain a major constraint. A truly global currency needs deep and liquid markets, confidence in the currency and relatively free movement of capital. China continues to manage capital flows and the exchange rate. The RMB is therefore becoming more important as a funding and settlement currency, but it still lacks the openness normally associated with a major reserve currency. Beijing is trying to open the door to global capital while keeping its hand on the lock.
Why It Matters
For investors, China's RMB push creates a new diversification opportunity as deeper RMB bond markets and financial infrastructure could make Chinese assets and China-linked businesses more relevant to global portfolios. But with currency risk, capital controls and liquidity constraints still in place, the right approach is to watch whether foreign participation and RMB demand continue to deepen before treating the RMB as a meaningful alternative funding and investment market.
