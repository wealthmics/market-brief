Global · Policy
Why Are Central Banks Holding Rates, and What Do the US Midterms Mean for Markets?
Central banks are maintaining a cautious stance as renewed energy price pressures complicate the inflation outlook, while the impact of earlier rate increases is still working its way through their economies.
Why Are Central Banks Holding Rates Despite a Hawkish Stance?
Inflation trends across Canada, the US and the Eurozone highlight the challenge facing policymakers as energy price pressures and geopolitical uncertainty complicate the outlook. Although inflation dynamics differ across these economies, the risk of higher energy and transportation costs feeding into broader prices remains a concern. Central banks must therefore remain cautious about easing policy too early, particularly while inflation has not been fully brought under control.
Source: Bank of Canada, October 2026 Monetary Policy Report
Despite maintaining a hawkish stance, major developed-market central banks have largely held interest rates rather than introducing further hikes. This reflects a balancing act: higher rates could help contain persistent inflation, but they could also weaken economic activity, while the effects of previous tightening are still filtering through to households and businesses. The current pause is therefore more about assessing incoming data and the impact of existing policy than signalling a clear shift towards monetary easing.
The rate pause reflects caution, not confidence that the inflation battle is over.
US Midterm Elections: Political Uncertainty and Market Performance
President Donald Trump’s approval rating has weakened considerably ahead of the November 3 midterm elections. According to a survey conducted between September 28 and October 4, 2026, his approval stands at 30%, down from approximately 47% in February. This is also below the roughly 38% approval recorded at a comparable point during his first presidential term. The declining approval reflects growing voter dissatisfaction and puts Republicans under pressure as they defend their narrow congressional majorities. The election therefore represents an important test of the administration’s political mandate, particularly around economic management and the cost of living.
What History Says About Markets After Midterms
Historical market data presents a more constructive picture. Since 1970, the S&P 500 has delivered an average total return of approximately 14.1% in the 126 trading days following midterm elections, compared with 5.7% during comparable periods in non-midterm years. Markets have historically performed better after midterms, potentially as political uncertainty clears and investors gain greater visibility into the policy environment. This pattern should not be read as a guaranteed outcome. Political uncertainty can create short-term volatility, but the broader market direction will ultimately depend on economic growth, corporate earnings, inflation and the Federal Reserve’s policy trajectory.
S&P 500 total return performance since 1970, indexed to midterm date.
Political uncertainty may dominate headlines, but history suggests that markets often look beyond election cycles.
Why It Matters
Major central banks remain hawkish, with interest rates largely on hold as policymakers balance persistent inflation risks against slowing economic growth. Monetary easing may not come quickly, keeping global financial conditions relatively tight. Meanwhile, the US midterm elections could reshape the balance of power in Washington, influencing fiscal priorities, taxation and regulation. Although history points to stronger equity performance after midterms, investors should stay attentive to policy uncertainty, inflation, interest rates and corporate earnings.
